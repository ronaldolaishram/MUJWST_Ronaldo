Project 3: Spatial Distribution & Environmental Effects

Probing Large-Scale Structure and Environmental Quenching
COSMOS-Web Workshop | Advanced Level Analysis | Duration: 30-35 minutes
Abstract: We investigate the spatial distribution of galaxies in the COSMOS field using JWST observations to identify overdensities and quantify environmental effects on galaxy evolution. Through nearest-neighbor density estimation and correlation analysis, we map the cosmic web at redshifts 1.0 < z < 1.5, identifying proto-clusters, groups, and voids. By measuring the dependence of galaxy properties on local density, we constrain environmental quenching mechanisms operating during the epoch when massive galaxy clusters were assembling and the field galaxy population was establishing present-day trends.

1. Theoretical Framework: Environment in Galaxy Evolution

The paradigm of hierarchical structure formation predicts that matter collapses into an interconnected network—the cosmic web—composed of dense nodes (clusters), connecting filaments, sheets, and vast underdense regions (voids). Galaxies form within dark matter halos embedded in this structure, and their evolution is inextricably linked to their environment.

Environmental effects on galaxies have been studied for decades, beginning with the morphology-density relation discovered by Dressler in 1980. Modern observations reveal that environment affects not just morphology but also star formation rate, gas content, stellar populations, chemical abundances, and AGN activity. These effects arise because physical processes operating preferentially in dense environments alter galaxy properties.

1.1 Physical Mechanisms of Environmental Quenching

Multiple mechanisms contribute to transforming galaxies as they enter dense environments. Understanding which processes dominate at different epochs and in different environments constitutes a major research frontier.

Key Environmental Processes:

  • Ram-pressure stripping: Galaxies moving through hot intracluster medium experience pressure that removes cold gas from their disks. This process is rapid, operating on timescales of 100-500 Myr, and is most effective in massive clusters with deep potential wells and hot X-ray-emitting gas.
  • Strangulation (starvation): As galaxies fall into groups and clusters, their hot gas halos are stripped or heated, cutting off the supply of cold gas from cosmic filaments. Galaxies continue forming stars using remaining disk gas but gradually fade as this reservoir is exhausted. Timescales are longer, 1-2 Gyr, producing gradual quenching.
  • Galaxy harassment: High-speed encounters in dense environments tidally disturb galaxy disks, driving turbulence and potentially triggering starbursts followed by quenching. This process is stochastic and affects primarily low-mass galaxies in intermediate-density regions.
  • Major mergers: While not strictly environmental, merger rates are enhanced in overdense regions. Mergers can trigger starbursts, transform morphologies, and fuel AGN that subsequently quench star formation. Effects are dramatic but brief.
  • Preprocessing in groups: Many cluster galaxies were members of groups before cluster infall. Environmental processes in groups may begin transforming galaxies before they enter clusters, complicating interpretation of cluster-specific effects.

1.2 Observational Signatures

Each mechanism leaves distinct observational signatures. Ram-pressure stripping produces asymmetric gas distributions and jellyfish morphologies. Strangulation produces galaxies with suppressed star formation but undisturbed morphologies. Harassment creates disturbed, irregular morphologies. By correlating galaxy properties with environment, we can infer which processes dominate.

The critical observable is the quiescent fraction as a function of local density. If environmental quenching operates efficiently, we expect higher quiescent fractions in denser regions independent of stellar mass. The shape of this relationship—whether linear, exponential, or threshold-like—constrains the physics of quenching.

2. Methodological Approach

2.1 Density Field Reconstruction

Measuring galaxy environment requires quantifying the local density field. We employ the Nth nearest neighbor technique, which adapts to local density variations and avoids the fixed-aperture biases that plague simple counts-in-cylinder methods.

For each galaxy, we measure the distance to its Nth nearest neighbor at similar redshift. Small distances indicate high local density; large distances indicate isolation. The surface density is then calculated as Σ = N / (π r²), where r is the Nth neighbor distance. We typically use N = 5-10 to balance noise reduction against spatial resolution.

🔬 Technical Advantages

The nearest-neighbor approach naturally adapts to the varying survey coverage and selection functions across the COSMOS field. It avoids defining arbitrary aperture sizes and provides continuous density measurements rather than discrete environment classifications. The method has been extensively validated against X-ray selected clusters and spectroscopic redshift surveys.

2.2 Redshift Slice Selection

Environmental studies require careful redshift selection. Too broad a slice introduces projection effects, mixing galaxies at different cosmic epochs with no physical connection. Too narrow a slice reduces statistics. We select 1.0 < z < 1.5, corresponding to comoving depth ~1.5 Gpc. This balances minimizing projections while maintaining sufficient galaxy counts.

This redshift range is scientifically optimal. At z ~ 1-1.5, we observe the Universe during a critical transition. Cosmic star formation was declining from its z ~ 2 peak. Massive galaxy clusters were in active assembly, accreting groups and field galaxies. Environmental quenching was becoming increasingly important. By studying this epoch, we capture environmental processes in action.

3. Expected Observational Results

3.1 Spatial Distribution and Clustering

Visual inspection of the galaxy distribution on the sky will immediately reveal non-uniformity. Galaxies cluster on scales from small groups (few hundred kpc) to large-scale filaments (tens of Mpc). We expect to identify:

Projected Structure Detection:

  • Proto-clusters (δ > 3σ): 5-10 significant overdensities containing 20-50+ galaxies within ~2-3 Mpc regions. These represent the progenitors of present-day massive clusters (M > 10¹⁴ M☉). The COSMOS field is known to contain several such structures, providing validation targets.
  • Galaxy groups (δ ~ 2σ): 20-40 smaller overdensities with 5-20 members. These correspond to dark matter halos with masses 10¹³-10¹⁴ M☉, the environment where many present-day cluster galaxies were preprocessed.
  • Filamentary structures: Elongated overdensities connecting high-density peaks. These trace the cosmic web skeleton where gas accretion occurs and galaxies migrate toward clusters.
  • Voids (δ < -1): Underdense regions occupying the majority of volume but containing few galaxies. These provide important comparison samples representing minimal environmental influence.

3.2 Overdensity Statistics

The distribution of overdensity values follows predictions from gravitational collapse. Most galaxies reside near the median density (δ ~ 0). The distribution has a tail toward high density representing rare, highly clustered regions. The shape of this distribution evolves with redshift as structure grows through gravitational amplification of initial perturbations.

We characterize the distribution using standard deviation σ_δ ~ 1.5-2.0. The skewness is positive, reflecting that high-density peaks are rarer and more extreme than low-density troughs. These statistical properties match N-body simulation predictions for cosmic structure at z ~ 1-1.5.

3.3 Environmental Dependence of Galaxy Properties

The central scientific result concerns how galaxy properties vary with environment. We measure:

  • Quiescent fraction vs density: We expect monotonic increase from ~10-20% in voids to 40-60% in proto-clusters. The slope and shape of this relation constrain quenching timescales and mechanisms.
  • Median stellar mass vs density: Slight increase with density, reflecting that massive galaxies preferentially populate overdense regions. This complicates separating mass and environmental effects.
  • Star formation rate distributions: In dense regions, the SFR distribution shifts toward lower values and shows a more prominent quiescent peak. The SFR of remaining star-forming galaxies may also be suppressed.
  • Specific SFR vs density: After controlling for mass, sSFR should decrease with density if environmental quenching operates. The magnitude of this effect quantifies environmental quenching strength.

3.4 The Star-Forming Main Sequence in Different Environments

A key diagnostic examines whether the SFMS itself depends on environment. Two scenarios are possible:

Scenario 1: SFMS Normalization Depends on Environment

If galaxies on the SFMS in dense regions have systematically lower SFRs at fixed mass, environmental processes suppress but do not fully quench star formation. This suggests mechanisms like reduced gas accretion or mild harassment operate on main sequence galaxies.

Scenario 2: SFMS is Universal, Quiescent Fraction Varies

If the SFMS shows identical normalization and scatter regardless of environment, with only the quiescent fraction varying, environmental processes act rapidly to remove galaxies from the sequence rather than gradually suppressing their activity. This favors rapid processes like ram-pressure stripping.

Current observations at z < 1 generally support Scenario 2, but at z ~ 1-1.5 the situation is less clear. Our JWST observations can definitively distinguish these scenarios at this critical epoch.

4. Disentangling Mass and Environmental Effects

4.1 The Fundamental Challenge

A major complication in environmental studies is that stellar mass correlates with environment. Massive galaxies preferentially reside in dense regions because they form in massive dark matter halos that collapse earliest and cluster most strongly. Therefore, observing higher quiescent fractions in dense regions could reflect either mass quenching (massive galaxies quench regardless of environment) or environmental quenching (environment causes quenching independent of mass).

To isolate environmental effects, we must compare galaxies of similar mass in different environments. We split the sample into narrow mass bins (Δlog M* ~ 0.3 dex) and measure quiescent fraction as a function of density within each bin. If environment provides additional quenching beyond mass effects, we should observe increasing quiescent fraction with density even at fixed mass.

4.2 Expected Trends

Based on local universe studies and intermediate-redshift observations, we expect:

  • At high masses (log M* > 11), both mass and environmental quenching are important. Quiescent fraction is already high (~60-80%) even in low-density regions due to mass quenching, but increases further in dense regions.
  • At intermediate masses (10 < log M* < 11), environmental effects are most apparent. These galaxies remain predominantly star-forming in the field but show substantially elevated quiescent fractions in groups and clusters.
  • At low masses (log M* < 10), both mass and environmental quenching are weak. These galaxies are predominantly star-forming regardless of environment, though environmental effects may still be detectable with sufficient statistics.

5. Systematic Effects and Validation

5.1 Redshift Uncertainties

Photometric redshifts have typical uncertainties σ_z/(1+z) ~ 0.02-0.05. For our z ~ 1-1.5 sample, this corresponds to ~30-75 Mpc comoving uncertainty. This is comparable to typical group/cluster sizes, introducing potential contamination between environments.

We mitigate this through ensemble statistics. While individual galaxies may be misclassified, the population statistics remain robust. Cross-validation with spectroscopic subsamples and comparison with X-ray detected clusters provides confidence in our environment measurements.

5.2 Projection Effects

Two-dimensional projections of three-dimensional structure can create apparent overdensities from chance alignments of unrelated foreground and background galaxies. This is particularly problematic for marginal (2-3σ) overdensities.

Our narrow redshift slice minimizes this effect. Additionally, we can test for projection by examining redshift distributions within apparent overdensities. True structures show peaked redshift distributions, while projections show flat or multimodal distributions.

5.3 Selection Function

The COSMOS field has varying depth across the survey area due to observational constraints. Density measurements could be biased if completeness varies spatially. We account for this by normalizing local densities by the median density in regions with similar depth.

6. Scientific Impact and Implications

6.1 Proto-Cluster Identification

Identifying and characterizing proto-clusters at z ~ 1-1.5 addresses fundamental questions about cluster assembly. When did clusters virialize? How rapidly did they accrete galaxies from the field? What is the relationship between cluster mass and galaxy properties at early times?

JWST's sensitivity allows complete sampling of proto-cluster galaxy populations down to lower masses than previous surveys. This enables measuring mass functions, luminosity functions, and scaling relations within forming clusters, providing direct constraints on cluster galaxy evolution models.

🔬 Connection to Ongoing Research

The COSMOS field hosts several well-studied overdensities at z ~ 0.8-3.0. Our analysis can be directly compared with existing multiwavelength observations (X-ray, submillimeter, radio) to validate structure identification. Cross-matching with spectroscopic surveys provides redshift confirmation for candidate proto-clusters.

This project's methodology applies directly to searching for high-redshift proto-clusters in new JWST deep fields, a major science goal for understanding the earliest stages of cluster assembly and environmental effects.

6.2 Quenching Mechanisms

The measured dependence of quiescent fraction on density directly constrains environmental quenching mechanisms. Different processes predict different relationships:

  • If ram-pressure stripping dominates, quenching should occur primarily in the densest, most massive clusters with hot X-ray halos. We would see threshold behavior—little environmental effect until a critical density, then rapid quenching.
  • If strangulation dominates, effects should extend to lower densities corresponding to groups. The relationship would be smoother, reflecting gradual gas depletion over Gyr timescales.
  • If preprocessing in groups is important, we should observe substantial quiescent fractions even in moderate overdensities (groups) before galaxies enter massive clusters.

By comparing our z ~ 1-1.5 results with local universe studies, we test whether environmental quenching mechanisms evolve with cosmic time. Changes could reflect evolution in cluster masses, intracluster medium properties, or the efficiency of gas accretion onto galaxies.

6.3 Mass vs Environment

Quantifying the relative importance of mass and environmental quenching at z ~ 1-1.5 addresses a central question in galaxy evolution. At z = 0, both processes clearly operate. But when did environmental quenching become important? Was it already significant at z > 2 when most massive galaxies were still forming stars, or did it emerge at z ~ 1 as clusters grew massive enough to host hot gas halos?

Our analysis provides direct empirical constraints by measuring environmental effects at fixed mass. If we find strong environmental dependence, it indicates that environment was already influencing galaxy evolution during the epoch of peak cluster assembly. Weak or absent environmental effects would suggest that environmental quenching is a more recent phenomenon.

7. Comparison with Theoretical Predictions

7.1 Hydrodynamic Simulations

Modern cosmological simulations (IllustrisTNG, EAGLE, SIMBA) include prescriptions for gas cooling, star formation, stellar feedback, and AGN feedback. These simulations make predictions for environmental dependence of galaxy properties that we can directly test.

Simulations generally reproduce the environmental dependence of quiescent fraction at z = 0, but predictions diverge at higher redshift. Some models over-quench in groups, producing too many passive galaxies at intermediate densities. Others under-quench, leaving too many star-forming galaxies in clusters. Our observations provide critical tests to distinguish between models.

7.2 Semi-Analytic Models

Semi-analytic models implement environmental processes through parameterized prescriptions. These models make explicit predictions for quenching timescales, mass dependence, and environmental dependence. By comparing observed quiescent fractions and SFR distributions with model predictions, we constrain the parameters governing environmental quenching in these frameworks.

8. Extended Analysis Opportunities

This foundational spatial analysis enables numerous follow-up investigations:

📊 Future Extensions

  • Morphology-density relation: Combining our environment measurements with morphological classifications from JWST imaging reveals when and how morphological transformation occurs.
  • Radial profiles: Measuring galaxy properties as functions of distance from cluster centers constrains where different environmental processes dominate.
  • Velocity dispersion measurements: Spectroscopic follow-up of identified overdensities measures velocity dispersions, enabling dynamical mass estimates and virial state determination.
  • Cross-correlation with X-ray and SZ: Matching photometric overdensities with X-ray or Sunyaev-Zeldovich detected clusters validates our identifications and enables joint optical-X-ray studies.
  • Higher redshift extension: Applying identical methodology to z ~ 2-3 samples traces environmental effect evolution across cosmic time.
  • Resolved star formation: JWST imaging enables measuring spatially resolved SFR indicators, revealing whether star formation is preferentially suppressed in galaxy outskirts (consistent with ram-pressure stripping) or centrally concentrated.

9. Pedagogical Significance

Beyond scientific outcomes, this project provides comprehensive training in spatial analysis techniques essential for modern extragalactic astronomy. Participants learn:

  • Density field reconstruction methods applicable to any galaxy survey
  • Statistical techniques for quantifying correlations between discrete variables
  • Visualization of spatial data and large-scale structure
  • Careful consideration of selection effects and systematic biases
  • Interpretation of environmental effects in the context of physical mechanisms

The clear visual results—maps showing galaxy clustering, plots revealing environmental quenching—make this project particularly effective for communicating how large surveys constrain galaxy evolution physics. The connection to ongoing research on high-redshift structure formation demonstrates the relevance of workshop skills to cutting-edge science.

10. Concluding Synthesis

Environmental effects represent a critical but complex aspect of galaxy evolution. Unlike intrinsic processes that depend solely on galaxy mass and history, environmental quenching requires galaxies to exist in specific locations within the cosmic web and to interact with their surroundings through gas physics and gravitational dynamics.

This project employs JWST COSMOS-Web observations to map the spatial distribution of galaxies at z ~ 1-1.5 and quantify how environment affects their star formation. Through systematic nearest-neighbor density measurements and correlation analysis, we identify proto-clusters, groups, and voids, then measure how quiescent fraction and star formation properties depend on local density.

The expected results—increasing quiescent fraction with density, environmental effects strongest at intermediate masses, proto-cluster identification—will provide direct observational constraints on environmental quenching mechanisms operating during a critical epoch. These measurements feed into broader efforts to construct a complete theory of galaxy evolution that incorporates both intrinsic and environmental drivers.

By completing Projects 1-3, workshop participants have explored the three pillars of galaxy evolution studies: intrinsic scaling relations (SFMS), stellar population classification (UVJ), and environmental effects (spatial analysis). Together, these analyses provide a comprehensive framework for understanding how galaxies form, evolve, and ultimately quench across cosmic time. The techniques and insights gained apply broadly across extragalactic astronomy, from local universe surveys to the highest-redshift JWST observations.

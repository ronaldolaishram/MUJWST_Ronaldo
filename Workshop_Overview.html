Workshop Overview

Today we explore the remarkable capabilities of JWST COSMOS-Web data to address fundamental questions in galaxy evolution. Our workshop focuses on three interconnected projects that reveal how galaxies form stars, evolve their stellar populations, and respond to their cosmic environment across billions of years of cosmic history.

We work with one of the largest and deepest JWST surveys covering the COSMOS field, providing unprecedented views of galaxies at redshifts 0.5 < z < 3.0. This corresponds to observing the Universe when it was 2-8 billion years old, a critical epoch when galaxies were actively building their stellar masses and establishing the structures we observe today.

Project 1: The Star-Forming Main Sequence

Beginner 20-25 minutes

Scientific Motivation

The star-forming main sequence (SFMS) represents one of the most fundamental empirical relationships in extragalactic astronomy. For over a decade, observations have shown that the majority of star-forming galaxies follow a tight correlation between their stellar mass and star formation rate. This relationship persists across cosmic time, though its normalization evolves with redshift.

Understanding this relationship tells us about the physical processes regulating star formation in galaxies. The existence of a main sequence suggests that galaxies maintain a relatively stable mode of star formation over long timescales, with systematic variations driven by either internal feedback mechanisms or external environmental influences.

What We Will Discover

Through this analysis, we quantify the SFMS in our COSMOS-Web sample. We measure the slope of the mass-SFR relation, which typically ranges from 0.8 to 1.0, and characterize the intrinsic scatter, typically around 0.3 dex. This scatter is not merely observational uncertainty—it represents real physical variation in how efficiently galaxies convert gas into stars.

We identify three distinct populations. Galaxies lying on the main sequence represent normal star-forming systems in quasi-equilibrium. Those above the sequence (starbursts) are undergoing enhanced star formation, possibly triggered by mergers or gas accretion events. Galaxies below the sequence may be transitioning toward quiescence, experiencing suppressed star formation due to gas depletion or feedback processes.

🔑 Key Physical Insights

The specific star formation rate (sSFR = SFR/M*) provides a characteristic timescale for galaxy growth. For main sequence galaxies at z~1-2, typical sSFR values of 10^-9 to 10^-10 yr^-1 imply doubling times of 1-10 Gyr. This means galaxies at cosmic noon were actively building their stellar mass on timescales comparable to the age of the Universe at that epoch.

Expected Results and Implications

Our COSMOS-Web analysis will reveal the SFMS with unprecedented statistics. With thousands of galaxies spanning three decades in stellar mass, we can probe the relationship down to low-mass systems that were previously inaccessible. We expect to find:

  • A clear linear relationship in log-log space with slope ~0.9, consistent with previous studies but with reduced uncertainty
  • Intrinsic scatter of 0.2-0.3 dex, reflecting galaxy-to-galaxy variation in star formation efficiency
  • Starburst fraction of 10-20%, representing galaxies undergoing transient enhanced star formation
  • Evidence for mass-dependent sSFR, with lower-mass galaxies showing higher specific growth rates

🌟 Scientific Impact

This project establishes the baseline for understanding normal galaxy evolution. By defining the main sequence in our sample, we create a reference frame for identifying galaxies with unusual star formation histories. This becomes crucial for Projects 2 and 3, where we investigate what causes galaxies to deviate from this sequence.

Connection to Current Research

The SFMS remains an active area of investigation. Recent studies debate whether the slope evolves with redshift, whether there is a turnover at high masses due to AGN feedback, and whether the sequence is truly universal or depends on environment. Our JWST data, with its superior sensitivity and resolution, allows us to address these questions with unprecedented clarity at z > 1.

Project 2: UVJ Color-Color Diagram

Intermediate 25-30 minutes

Scientific Motivation

Galaxy colors encode the integrated light from stellar populations and are therefore powerful diagnostics of star formation history and dust content. However, a single color can be degenerate—red galaxies could be either old and passive or young and dusty. The UVJ technique, pioneered by Williams et al. (2009), breaks this degeneracy by using two colors simultaneously.

The U-V color is sensitive to the 4000Å break and thus traces the mean age of stellar populations. Young, star-forming galaxies with hot blue stars appear blue in U-V, while old passive galaxies with evolved stellar populations appear red. The V-J color is more sensitive to dust reddening than to stellar age. By combining these diagnostics in a two-dimensional color space, we can cleanly separate truly quiescent galaxies from dusty star-forming systems.

Methodology and Approach

We utilize rest-frame absolute magnitudes from LePhare SED fitting. While we use M_NUV as a proxy for U-band and M_r as a proxy for V-band, these provide excellent approximations for the canonical UVJ selection. The power of this method lies in its ability to work with photometric data alone, without requiring spectroscopy.

The Williams et al. (2009) criteria define a region in UVJ space where quiescent galaxies reside. This empirical selection has been validated through extensive comparison with spectroscopic classifications, morphologies, and direct SFR measurements. Galaxies satisfying (U-V) > 1.3, (V-J) < 1.6, and (U-V) > 0.88(V-J) + 0.49 are classified as quiescent.

🔑 Physical Interpretation of Color Space

The blue cloud in UVJ space represents actively star-forming galaxies with young stellar populations and modest dust content. The red sequence contains passive galaxies dominated by old stellar populations with minimal ongoing star formation. Importantly, dusty star-forming galaxies—which would appear red in optical colors—are correctly identified as star-forming because their V-J colors reveal dust reddening.

Expected Discoveries

Through this analysis, we will observe the bimodality in galaxy properties that has been recognized for decades but can now be quantified with JWST precision. We expect to find:

  • Clear separation between star-forming and quiescent populations, with typically 20-30% quiescent fraction at z~1-2
  • Strong mass dependence in quiescent fraction, rising from <10% at M* < 10^10 M☉ to >50% at M* > 10^11 M☉
  • Excellent agreement (>85%) between UVJ classification and sSFR-based classification for most galaxies
  • Small population of discrepant objects, including recently quenched galaxies and heavily dust-obscured starbursts

Implications for Galaxy Evolution

The mass-dependent quiescent fraction is one of the most important observational constraints on galaxy evolution models. That massive galaxies are more likely to be quiescent suggests that mass (or correlated properties like halo mass) plays a fundamental role in shutting down star formation. This "mass quenching" or "downsizing" phenomenon indicates that the Universe builds galaxies from the top down, with massive systems ceasing star formation earlier than low-mass systems.

🌟 Breakthrough Potential

With JWST's sensitivity, we can push UVJ analysis to lower masses and higher redshifts than ever before. This allows us to trace when mass quenching becomes important in cosmic history and whether the UVJ boundaries are truly universal or evolve with redshift. Deviations from established trends could indicate new physics in galaxy quenching mechanisms.

Cross-validation with Physical Properties

We validate our UVJ classification by comparing with direct SFR measurements from SED fitting. The correlation between color-based and SFR-based classification demonstrates the robustness of the technique. Disagreements between methods highlight interesting transitional populations—the "green valley" galaxies that are in the process of quenching or rejuvenating their star formation.

Project 3: Spatial Distribution & Environmental Effects

Advanced 30-35 minutes

Scientific Motivation

Galaxy evolution does not occur in isolation. The large-scale structure of the Universe—the cosmic web of filaments, clusters, and voids—profoundly influences how galaxies form and evolve. Environmental effects have been recognized since the 1970s when Dressler documented the density-morphology relation: elliptical galaxies preferentially reside in dense cluster cores while spiral galaxies dominate lower-density regions.

Modern studies have refined this picture, showing that environment affects not just morphology but also star formation rate, gas content, and chemical composition. The mechanisms driving environmental quenching—ram-pressure stripping, strangulation, harassment, and preprocessing—leave distinct observational signatures that we can detect through statistical analysis of galaxy clustering and properties.

Analytical Framework

We employ a nearest-neighbor density estimator to characterize the local environment of each galaxy. This technique measures the distance to the Nth nearest neighbor and uses this to calculate surface density. The advantage over fixed-aperture methods is that it adapts to the local galaxy distribution, providing higher sensitivity to both overdense and underdense regions.

By working in a narrow redshift slice (z = 1.0-1.5), we minimize projection effects while maintaining sufficient statistics. This slice corresponds to a critical epoch in cosmic history—the era of peak cosmic star formation activity when galaxy clusters were still assembling and environmental processes were beginning to significantly impact galaxy evolution.

🔑 Understanding Cosmic Structure

The cosmic web emerges naturally from gravitational collapse of initial density perturbations. Dark matter halos collapse first in overdense regions, creating potential wells where baryonic matter accumulates to form galaxies. The most massive halos become galaxy clusters, containing hundreds to thousands of galaxies. Between clusters, galaxies trace filamentary structures. In underdense regions (voids), galaxy formation is suppressed.

Expected Environmental Signatures

Our spatial analysis will reveal the hierarchical structure of galaxy distribution. We anticipate identifying multiple scales of clustering:

  • Proto-clusters: overdensities with δ > 3σ containing 20-50+ galaxies, representing the progenitors of present-day massive clusters
  • Galaxy groups: smaller overdensities with δ ~ 2σ, containing 5-20 galaxies in gravitationally bound systems
  • Filamentary structures: elongated overdensities connecting high-density peaks
  • Voids: underdense regions with δ < -1, containing few if any galaxies

Environmental Quenching Analysis

The most powerful aspect of this project is correlating environment with galaxy properties. We measure the quiescent fraction as a function of local density, expecting to find a monotonic increase from field to cluster environments. This relationship is the observational signature of environmental quenching.

Multiple physical processes contribute to environmental quenching. In dense environments, galaxies move at high velocities through the hot intracluster medium, experiencing ram-pressure that strips their cold gas reservoirs. Even without direct stripping, accretion of cold gas from the cosmic web can be cut off (strangulation), causing galaxies to passively exhaust their gas supply. High-speed galaxy encounters (harassment) can tidally disrupt gas disks. These processes work in concert to transform star-forming field galaxies into passive cluster members.

🌟 Research Frontiers

This analysis directly connects to cutting-edge questions in galaxy evolution. At z ~ 1-1.5, we observe environmental quenching in action. Unlike at z = 0 where we see the end result, or at z > 2 where environment effects are weaker, this epoch captures the transition. We can test whether environmental quenching operates similarly across cosmic time or whether new processes become important as clusters virialize and grow.

Disentangling Mass and Environment

A critical challenge is separating mass quenching from environmental quenching. Since massive galaxies preferentially reside in dense environments, we observe a correlation between mass, environment, and quiescent fraction. To isolate environmental effects, we compare galaxies of similar mass in different environments. We expect to find that environment provides additional quenching beyond what mass alone predicts, particularly for intermediate-mass galaxies (10^10 < M* < 10^11 M☉) where both processes contribute significantly.

Connection to Ongoing Research

This project employs techniques directly relevant to current research on high-redshift structure. Proto-cluster identification at z > 1 is a major science goal for JWST. The COSMOS field is known to contain significant overdensities, and JWST's depth allows us to detect their member galaxies with unprecedented completeness. Understanding how environmental processes operate in these forming clusters informs models of cluster assembly and galaxy evolution.

Our analysis of environmental quenching connects to the broader question of how galaxy populations establish the bimodality we observe locally. The red sequence and blue cloud are not fixed populations but rather reflect the balance between star-forming and quenching timescales. Environment modulates this balance, accelerating quenching in dense regions while allowing extended star formation in the field.

Systematic Validation

We validate our environmental measurements by examining whether the SFMS itself depends on environment. Some theoretical models predict that environmental processes suppress star formation on the main sequence before fully quenching galaxies, leading to a lower normalization of the SFMS in dense regions. Alternatively, quenching might be rapid, removing galaxies from the SFMS without affecting those that remain. Our data can distinguish these scenarios by measuring whether the scatter and slope of the SFMS varies with density.

Project 2: UVJ Color-Color Diagram

Photometric Classification of Star-Forming and Quiescent Galaxies
COSMOS-Web Workshop | Intermediate Level Analysis | Duration: 25-30 minutes
Abstract: We employ rest-frame UVJ color selection to classify COSMOS-Web galaxies into star-forming and quiescent populations. This technique, validated across multiple surveys and redshift ranges, separates galaxies based on stellar population age and dust content. Through systematic comparison with SFR-derived classifications and analysis of the mass-dependent quiescent fraction, we probe the transformation of galaxies from active star formation to passive evolution. The unprecedented depth and resolution of JWST observations enable extension of this analysis to lower masses and higher redshifts than previously accessible.

1. Historical Context and Theoretical Framework

Galaxy bimodality—the division between actively star-forming and passive systems—has been recognized since the 1970s but gained quantitative rigor with large spectroscopic surveys in the early 2000s. The SDSS revealed a clear color bimodality in the local universe, with galaxies populating either a blue cloud or red sequence with relatively few intermediate systems.

However, simple optical colors suffer from the dust-age degeneracy. A galaxy can appear red either because it hosts predominantly old stellar populations (truly quiescent) or because ongoing star formation is heavily obscured by dust. This degeneracy severely limits the reliability of single-color classifications, particularly at high redshift where dusty star formation becomes increasingly prevalent.

1.1 The UVJ Breakthrough

Williams et al. (2009) demonstrated that combining rest-frame U-V and V-J colors breaks this degeneracy. The U-V color is sensitive to the 4000Å break, which strengthens as stellar populations age. Young, star-forming galaxies with hot O and B stars are blue in U-V, while old passive galaxies dominated by K giants are red. Crucially, dust affects U-V but is wavelength-dependent.

The V-J color provides the crucial second dimension. Dust reddening affects shorter wavelengths more strongly, so dusty star-forming galaxies become very red in V-J. Old passive galaxies, having little dust, show more moderate V-J colors. By examining both colors simultaneously, we cleanly separate three populations: blue star-forming galaxies, dusty star-forming galaxies, and genuinely quiescent galaxies.

Williams et al. (2009) Quiescent Selection Criteria:

(U - V) > 1.3
(V - J) < 1.6
(U - V) > 0.88 × (V - J) + 0.49

1.2 Physical Interpretation of Color Space

The rest-frame U-band (~3650 Å) captures the UV continuum from young massive stars and is strongly suppressed in old populations. The V-band (~5500 Å) sits near the 4000Å break, while J-band (~1.25 μm) probes the stellar mass-to-light ratio minimum dominated by intermediate-age stars. The combination provides a compact encoding of the integrated star formation history.

Stellar population synthesis models predict specific trajectories through UVJ space as galaxies evolve. A galaxy forming stars at constant rate occupies the blue cloud. If star formation ceases instantaneously, the galaxy moves upward and rightward in the diagram as the stellar population ages and becomes redder. Dusty starburst galaxies move horizontally rightward, becoming red in V-J while maintaining relatively blue U-V colors beneath the dust.

2. Observational Implementation and Systematics

2.1 Rest-Frame Magnitude Estimation

Our analysis utilizes rest-frame absolute magnitudes from LePhare SED fitting. We approximate U-band with M_NUV (near-UV), V-band with M_r (SDSS r-band), and use M_J directly. While not exact matches to the canonical UVJ filters, these provide excellent proxies validated in numerous studies.

The SED fitting process models each galaxy's spectral energy distribution using stellar population synthesis models, determining the best-fit redshift, stellar mass, dust attenuation, and star formation history. Rest-frame magnitudes are then interpolated or extrapolated from the best-fit model. This approach is superior to k-corrections of observed magnitudes because it accounts for spectral features and dust.

🔬 Methodological Advantages

LePhare's chi-squared minimization approach fits the entire SED simultaneously, providing self-consistent physical parameters. The Bayesian marginalization over redshift probability distributions reduces random errors. Multi-band JWST photometry spanning 0.9-4.4 μm provides excellent constraints on rest-frame optical and near-infrared colors even at z > 2.

2.2 Expected Systematic Uncertainties

Several systematic effects warrant consideration. The choice of stellar population synthesis model (Bruzual-Charlot, FSPS, etc.) affects rest-frame colors at the ~0.1 magnitude level. Dust attenuation law assumptions introduce additional scatter. We mitigate these systematics through ensemble statistics rather than individual galaxy classifications.

The proxy filters (NUV for U, r for V) introduce offsets but preserve the essential color-color topology. Empirical tests comparing true UVJ selection with our proxy selection show agreement at the 90-95% level, sufficient for statistical studies. The quiescent selection boundaries may require slight empirical adjustment based on comparison with sSFR distributions.

3. Projected Observational Results

3.1 UVJ Distribution and Bimodality

We anticipate clear bimodality in the UVJ diagram, with distinct concentrations corresponding to star-forming and quiescent populations. The star-forming cloud should occupy the region with (U-V) < 1.3, extending from blue (dustless) to red (dusty) V-J colors. The quiescent sequence occupies the wedge defined by the Williams criteria, with typical (U-V) ~ 1.5-2.0 and (V-J) ~ 0.5-1.5.

Projected Population Statistics (z = 0.5-3.0):

  • Star-forming galaxies: 70-75% of the sample. These span a wide range in U-V color reflecting varying star formation histories and dust content, but generally have (U-V) < 1.3.
  • Quiescent galaxies: 20-25% of the sample meeting the Williams criteria. These show tight clustering in color space, indicating relatively homogeneous stellar populations dominated by old stars with minimal ongoing star formation.
  • Green valley: 5-10% lying near the boundary. These transitional galaxies are either recently quenched or rejuvenating, providing insights into transformation timescales.

3.2 Mass-Dependent Quiescent Fraction

One of the most robust results in galaxy evolution is the strong mass dependence of the quiescent fraction. We project the following relationship across our mass range:

  • At log(M*/M☉) ~ 9.5: f_quiescent ~ 5-10%, indicating that low-mass galaxies remain predominantly star-forming
  • At log(M*/M☉) ~ 10.5: f_quiescent ~ 20-30%, the transition regime where both star-forming and quiescent galaxies are common
  • At log(M*/M☉) ~ 11.5: f_quiescent ~ 50-70%, showing that massive galaxies are predominantly quiescent

This mass dependence encodes fundamental physics. Massive galaxies reside in more massive dark matter halos where multiple quenching mechanisms operate efficiently. AGN feedback scales with black hole mass, which correlates with stellar mass. Gravitational heating of infalling gas (virial shock heating) becomes effective above critical halo masses. Additionally, massive galaxies formed earlier and have had longer to quench.

3.3 Comparison with SFR-Based Classification

We validate UVJ classification by comparing with sSFR-based quenching criteria (typically log(sSFR) < -11 yr⁻¹). Agreement between methods should exceed 85%, demonstrating that photometric classification reliably identifies quiescent galaxies without requiring spectroscopy or resolved star formation indicators.

🔍 Discrepant Objects

The ~10-15% of galaxies classified differently by UVJ and sSFR methods are scientifically interesting. UVJ-quiescent but sSFR-starforming objects may be recently quenched galaxies where the integrated stellar population remains blue but current star formation has ceased. Conversely, UVJ-starforming but sSFR-quiescent galaxies could be heavily dust-obscured systems or galaxies with bursty star formation histories.

4. Physical Mechanisms and Interpretation

4.1 Mass Quenching Paradigm

The strong mass dependence of quiescent fraction suggests that galaxy-scale processes drive quenching. Several mechanisms operate more efficiently in massive systems. AGN feedback, powered by accretion onto supermassive black holes, can heat or expel gas from galactic halos. Since black hole mass scales with stellar mass, AGN feedback is naturally more powerful in massive galaxies.

Additionally, massive galaxies inhabit deeper potential wells with higher virial temperatures. When the halo virial temperature exceeds ~10⁶ K, infalling gas becomes shock-heated and cannot efficiently cool and accrete onto the galaxy. This "hot halo" quenching mode may explain why the most massive galaxies become quiescent even in isolated field environments.

4.2 Timescales of Transformation

The green valley—the region between star-forming and quiescent in color space—provides insights into quenching timescales. A narrow green valley indicates rapid transitions (<1 Gyr), while a populated green valley suggests slower evolution. Our UVJ analysis quantifies the green valley population, constraining whether quenching typically occurs rapidly through violent processes or gradually through secular evolution.

Spectroscopic observations of green valley galaxies reveal diverse properties. Some show AGN signatures, suggesting feedback-driven quenching. Others have disturbed morphologies indicating recent mergers. Still others appear undisturbed, perhaps experiencing gradual gas exhaustion. The fraction in each category constrains the relative importance of different quenching channels.

5. Redshift Evolution and Cosmic Context

5.1 Evolution of Quiescent Fraction

While this project analyzes the combined z = 0.5-3.0 sample, the data enable subdividing into redshift bins to trace evolution. We expect the overall quiescent fraction to decline with increasing redshift, consistent with the downsizing paradigm. At z ~ 2-3, only the most massive galaxies (log M* > 11) should show significant quiescent fractions, while lower-mass systems remain universally star-forming.

This evolution reflects the cosmic star formation history. The Universe-averaged star formation rate density peaks at z ~ 2 and declines by an order of magnitude to z = 0. This decline results from the increasing quiescent fraction at all masses, the declining star formation rates of remaining star-forming galaxies, and the declining number density of newly formed galaxies.

5.2 Assembly of the Red Sequence

The red sequence—the tight locus occupied by quiescent galaxies in color-magnitude space—builds up over cosmic time. At z > 2, few galaxies populate the red sequence and those present are predominantly very massive. By z ~ 1, the red sequence is well-established across a wider mass range. At z = 0, even moderately massive galaxies (log M* ~ 10.5) have substantial quiescent fractions.

This progressive build-up of the red sequence from high masses to lower masses over time constitutes observational evidence for downsizing. Massive galaxies quenched earliest and have remained passive, while lower-mass galaxies continued forming stars to more recent epochs. This mass-dependent evolution challenges simple hierarchical models where small galaxies form first.

6. Systematic Validation and Robustness Tests

6.1 Comparison Across Selection Methods

To validate our UVJ classification, we compare with alternative quenching indicators. Beyond sSFR, we examine:

  • Distance from SFMS: Quiescent galaxies should lie >1σ below the main sequence established in Project 1.
  • SED chi-squared: Quiescent galaxies should be well-fit by old stellar population templates with low chi-squared values.
  • CIGALE parameters: Cross-check with CIGALE-derived stellar ages; quiescent galaxies should show ages >2-3 Gyr.

Consistency across multiple independent indicators provides confidence that our classifications reflect real physical differences rather than artifacts of a particular measurement technique.

⚠️ Potential Systematics

We must remain vigilant about selection effects. Extremely dust-obscured star-forming galaxies might be misclassified if their SEDs are poorly constrained. Post-starburst galaxies, having ceased star formation within the past ~500 Myr, have intermediate colors and may scatter between classifications. Photo-z errors can shift rest-frame colors and affect classification. These effects become more severe at higher redshifts where photometric constraints weaken.

7. Scientific Impact and Broader Context

7.1 Constraints on Galaxy Evolution Models

The mass-dependent quiescent fraction is a primary observable for testing galaxy formation models. Semi-analytic models and hydrodynamic simulations make predictions about when and how efficiently galaxies quench as functions of mass, halo mass, and environment. Discrepancies between models and observations indicate missing or incorrectly implemented physics.

Current models struggle to reproduce the observed quiescent fractions across all mass and redshift ranges simultaneously. Some models over-quench low-mass galaxies, producing too many faint red galaxies. Others under-quench massive galaxies at high redshift, forming too many z > 2 star-forming galaxies above 10¹¹ M☉. Our JWST measurements provide stringent tests to discriminate between models.

7.2 Connection to Subsequent Analysis

The UVJ classification feeds directly into Project 3's environmental analysis. By separating star-forming and quiescent populations, we can measure the environmental dependence of quiescent fraction. If environment matters, we expect higher quiescent fractions in dense regions independent of mass. This disentangles mass quenching from environmental quenching.

Furthermore, identifying quiescent galaxies enables targeted follow-up studies. Spectroscopy can measure velocity dispersions to estimate dynamical masses, validate stellar mass estimates, and search for residual star formation or AGN activity. Morphological analysis can determine whether quiescent galaxies have elliptical or S0 morphologies, constraining formation mechanisms.

8. Broader Implications for Galaxy Evolution Theory

8.1 The Quenching Problem

Understanding why and how galaxies stop forming stars remains one of the central questions in extragalactic astronomy. Naive expectations based solely on gas supply suggest that galaxies should form stars continuously as they accrete material from the cosmic web. Yet we observe that roughly one-third of massive galaxies at z ~ 1 are quiescent, indicating that powerful quenching mechanisms operate.

The UVJ technique provides an efficient census of quiescent galaxies across cosmic time. By measuring when galaxies quenched (through their stellar population ages) and comparing with dynamical timescales, we constrain the rapidity of quenching. Fast quenching implicates violent processes like AGN outflows, while slow quenching suggests gradual gas exhaustion or shutdown of accretion.

8.2 The Role of Stellar Feedback

While AGN feedback receives much attention for quenching massive galaxies, stellar feedback—supernova-driven winds—likely dominates at lower masses. The transition mass where AGN feedback becomes important may be encoded in the shape of the quiescent fraction versus mass relation. If this relation shows a sharp transition around log M* ~ 10.5, it may indicate the black hole mass threshold where AGN feedback becomes efficient.

9. Future Directions and Extensions

Several natural extensions build on this foundational UVJ analysis. Subdividing by redshift traces the evolution of quiescent fraction and tests whether the UVJ selection boundaries remain fixed or evolve. Investigating whether morphology correlates with UVJ classification tests structure-SFH connections.

Cross-matching with X-ray catalogs identifies AGN among quiescent galaxies, probing whether AGN activity correlates with recent quenching. Spectroscopic follow-up measures metallicities and α-enhancement, constraining star formation timescales. Resolved photometry from JWST imaging maps color gradients, revealing inside-out or outside-in quenching signatures.

🚀 Advanced Research Opportunities

  • Machine learning classification trained on spectroscopic samples to refine photometric selection
  • Bayesian inference of star formation histories for individual galaxies using full photometric constraints
  • Comparison with IllustrisTNG or EAGLE simulations to identify model discrepancies
  • Environmental dependence of UVJ distributions in Project 3 integration

10. Conclusion

The UVJ color-color diagram remains one of the most powerful and efficient tools for classifying galaxy populations. Despite its simplicity—requiring only three rest-frame photometric measurements—it cleanly separates star-forming and quiescent galaxies while accounting for dust obscuration. This project demonstrates the technique's application to JWST COSMOS-Web data, extending previous studies to fainter magnitudes and enabling more complete mass-limited samples.

Our projected results will quantify the mass-dependent quiescent fraction with unprecedented precision. These measurements directly constrain galaxy evolution models and provide essential context for interpreting other observables. By establishing which galaxies are actively forming stars versus those that have quenched, we create the framework for understanding what drives this fundamental transformation in galaxy evolution.

The marriage of JWST's observational capabilities with proven analysis techniques promises to resolve long-standing questions about galaxy quenching while undoubtedly revealing new puzzles. As we probe fainter and more distant populations, we may discover that our current understanding of galaxy bimodality, forged primarily from z < 1 observations, requires revision for the higher-redshift Universe where JWST now provides unprecedented access.

Project 1: The Star-Forming Main Sequence

Exploring the Fundamental Scaling Relation of Galaxy Evolution
COSMOS-Web Workshop | Beginner Level Analysis | Duration: 20-25 minutes
Abstract: We investigate the star-forming main sequence (SFMS) using JWST COSMOS-Web observations spanning redshifts 0.5 < z < 3.0. This project quantifies the relationship between stellar mass and star formation rate for thousands of galaxies, providing empirical constraints on the processes regulating star formation across cosmic time. Through statistical analysis and population decomposition, we identify distinct evolutionary states and measure the characteristic timescales of galaxy growth during the epoch of peak cosmic star formation.

1. Scientific Context and Motivation

The star-forming main sequence emerges as a fundamental organizing principle in galaxy evolution. Since its recognition in large spectroscopic surveys of the local universe and subsequent confirmation at higher redshifts through multi-wavelength observations, the SFMS has become central to our understanding of how galaxies regulate their star formation over cosmic time.

The physical interpretation of the main sequence connects to the balance between gas accretion, star formation, and feedback. Galaxies on the sequence maintain quasi-equilibrium between gas supply and star formation, modulated by stellar and AGN feedback that prevents runaway star formation. This equilibrium is not static but evolves with cosmic time as gas accretion rates decline and feedback processes become more efficient in massive halos.

1.1 Current State of Knowledge

Observations from Herschel, ALMA, and previous HST surveys established that the SFMS follows a power-law relation with slope close to unity and scatter of approximately 0.3 dex. The normalization evolves with redshift, with galaxies at z ~ 2 forming stars more rapidly at fixed mass than local counterparts. However, uncertainties remain regarding the precise slope at high masses, potential flattening due to AGN feedback, and the universality of the relation across different environments.

🔭 JWST Advantage

JWST's superior sensitivity and resolution allow us to extend SFMS measurements to lower masses and probe the relationship with reduced selection biases. The infrared capabilities are particularly crucial for dust-obscured star formation, ensuring we capture the complete census of star-forming activity.

2. Expected Observational Results

2.1 Main Sequence Characterization

Our analysis of the COSMOS-Web catalog will yield the following quantitative measurements. The SFMS slope in the mass range 9.0 < log(M*/M☉) < 11.5 should be approximately 0.85 ± 0.05, slightly sub-linear indicating that star formation efficiency decreases with increasing mass. This sub-linearity has implications for galaxy growth rates and the evolution of the stellar mass function.

log(SFR) = α log(M*) + β(z)

where α ≈ 0.85 and β increases with redshift

The intrinsic scatter about the main sequence, after accounting for observational uncertainties, will be approximately 0.25-0.30 dex. This scatter is physically meaningful, reflecting real variation in star formation histories even among galaxies of similar mass at similar epochs. Understanding what drives this scatter—whether stochastic gas accretion, merger-driven bursts, or secular processes—remains an active research question.

2.2 Population Decomposition

We classify galaxies based on their offset from the main sequence (Δ_MS = log(SFR) - log(SFR_MS)). This approach identifies three populations with distinct physical interpretations:

Projected Population Fractions (z = 0.5-3.0):

  • Main Sequence galaxies (|Δ_MS| < 0.3 dex): 65-75% of the sample. These represent normal star-forming systems in quasi-equilibrium, steadily building stellar mass through sustained star formation.
  • Starburst galaxies (Δ_MS > 0.3 dex): 10-15% of the sample. Elevated SFR relative to mass suggests enhanced gas supply or increased star formation efficiency, possibly triggered by mergers or cosmic web accretion.
  • Below-sequence galaxies (Δ_MS < -0.3 dex): 15-20% of the sample. Suppressed SFR may indicate gas depletion, feedback-driven gas heating, or transitional states toward quiescence.

2.3 Specific Star Formation Rates

The specific star formation rate (sSFR = SFR/M*) provides a characteristic doubling time for stellar mass. At z ~ 1-2, we expect median log(sSFR) ~ -9.5 to -9.0 yr⁻¹, corresponding to doubling times of 3-10 Gyr. Crucially, sSFR shows systematic trends with both mass and redshift.

Lower-mass galaxies exhibit higher sSFR at fixed redshift, a manifestation of "downsizing" where massive galaxies formed earlier and are now growing more slowly. The sSFR also increases with redshift at fixed mass, reflecting higher gas accretion rates and cosmic gas fractions in the younger universe. These trends encode the assembly history of galaxy populations and constrain models of gas accretion and feedback.

3. Physical Interpretation Framework

3.1 Starburst Trigger Mechanisms

Galaxies above the main sequence require explanation. Several physical processes can elevate star formation rates above equilibrium values. Major mergers are well-known triggers, compressing gas and driving it toward galaxy centers where it rapidly forms stars. However, merger-driven starbursts should be relatively rare at z < 2, suggesting additional mechanisms.

Recent theoretical work emphasizes cold gas accretion along cosmic web filaments. Simulations show that gas streaming along filaments can penetrate hot halos in massive galaxies, providing fuel for enhanced star formation without requiring mergers. This "cold mode" accretion is expected to be particularly important at z ~ 1-2, precisely our target epoch.

3.2 Quenching Pathways

Galaxies below the main sequence may be caught in the act of quenching. Two primary pathways exist: rapid quenching that removes galaxies from the sequence on timescales shorter than observational cadence, and slow quenching where galaxies gradually drift below the sequence over several Gyr.

The relative importance of these pathways has implications for the mechanisms driving quenching. AGN feedback is often invoked for rapid quenching, explosively heating or expelling gas. Environmental processes like ram-pressure stripping can also act rapidly. Conversely, strangulation—the gradual shutoff of gas supply—produces slow quenching. Our measurement of the below-sequence population fraction constrains the prevalence of these mechanisms.

4. Systematic Uncertainties and Validation

4.1 SED Fitting Systematics

Our SFR and stellar mass estimates derive from spectral energy distribution fitting using LePhare. Systematic uncertainties arise from assumptions about star formation histories, dust attenuation laws, and stellar population synthesis models. We mitigate these by comparing LePhare results with CIGALE measurements, which use different SED fitting approaches.

Discrepancies between LePhare and CIGALE masses typically scatter around 0.1-0.2 dex, smaller than the intrinsic SFMS scatter. SFR estimates show larger variation, particularly for dusty galaxies where different attenuation assumptions produce different results. This motivates our focus on statistical measurements rather than individual galaxies.

4.2 Sample Selection Effects

Our mass cut at log(M*) > 9.0 ensures completeness but limits access to the low-mass regime where sSFR trends are most pronounced. The redshift range 0.5 < z < 3.0 encompasses significant cosmic evolution, so we must be cautious about interpreting ensemble measurements without accounting for redshift dependence.

Additionally, dust-obscured star formation poses challenges. While JWST's infrared sensitivity helps, extremely dusty galaxies may still be underrepresented. Cross-checking our SFMS against submillimeter-detected sources provides validation that we capture the full dynamic range of star formation rates.

5. Scientific Impact and Future Directions

5.1 Immediate Outcomes

This project establishes the empirical SFMS for our COSMOS-Web sample, providing the reference frame for subsequent analyses. By quantifying the sequence and its scatter, we enable systematic studies of what drives galaxies above or below the relation. The population fractions inform our understanding of galaxy evolutionary timescales and transition rates.

📊 Deliverables

  • Robust SFMS fit with slope, normalization, and scatter measurements
  • Classification catalog identifying main sequence, starburst, and below-sequence populations
  • sSFR distributions as functions of mass and redshift
  • Statistical framework for identifying unusual star formation histories

5.2 Broader Implications

Beyond the immediate measurements, the SFMS analysis connects to fundamental questions in galaxy evolution. The existence and tightness of the sequence constrains theoretical models of feedback and gas regulation. The scatter and its evolution with redshift test predictions from hydrodynamic simulations about the timescales of star formation fluctuations.

Deviations from the canonical SFMS at high masses may reveal AGN feedback signatures, while variations at low masses could reflect the stochasticity of star formation in small galaxies. Environmental dependence of the SFMS, explored in Project 3, tests whether galaxy-scale processes alone determine star formation or if larger-scale environment plays a role.

5.3 Extensions and Follow-up

Several natural extensions emerge from this foundational analysis. Splitting the sample into finer redshift bins reveals the evolution of the SFMS normalization, slope, and scatter across cosmic time. Investigating whether the sequence depends on morphology tests the connection between structure and star formation.

Comparison with resolved star formation rate indicators from JWST imaging could validate integrated SED-based measurements and reveal spatial variations in star formation efficiency. Spectroscopic follow-up of outliers—galaxies far above or below the sequence—would elucidate the physical conditions in these extreme systems.

6. Pedagogical Value

Beyond scientific outcomes, this project provides essential training in extragalactic data analysis. Participants gain experience with quality control, statistical characterization of galaxy populations, and interpretation of empirical scaling relations. The clear visual results—density plots revealing the main sequence, classifications showing distinct populations—make this an ideal introduction to galaxy evolution studies.

Understanding the SFMS is prerequisite knowledge for any galaxy evolution researcher. This project grounds theoretical concepts in real data, demonstrating how large surveys constrain physical models. The analysis pipeline developed here—from catalog loading through quality cuts to population statistics—forms a template applicable to diverse galaxy samples.

7. Conclusion

The star-forming main sequence represents the backbone of galaxy evolution studies. Through this COSMOS-Web analysis, we not only measure the relationship with unprecedented precision but also use it as a diagnostic tool to identify galaxies in different evolutionary states. The combination of JWST's observational capabilities with robust SED-derived physical parameters enables us to push these measurements to new regimes in mass and redshift space.

Our projected results will contribute to the growing body of evidence that galaxy star formation is regulated by systematic processes operating across cosmic time. The existence of a tight main sequence with well-defined scatter suggests that despite the complexity of baryonic physics—star formation, feedback, gas dynamics—galaxy evolution follows predictable patterns that we can observe, measure, and ultimately understand.
